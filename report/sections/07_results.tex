Accordingly, \cref{tab:main-results} and \cref{fig:accuracy-latency} focus on the \xml family and report speedup relative to the full-\xml validation sweep.

\subsection{Accuracy-Latency Trade-off}
Among the transcript-only front-end options, weighted fusion with $\alpha = 0.25$ is the best validation retriever, but its gains over dense-only retrieval are small. That result matters mostly because it confirms that the main systems leverage is not in squeezing out another fraction of a point from subtitle-window ranking. The more important question is what happens once the chosen retriever controls how much work \xml must do downstream.

The full multimodal \xml baseline exposes the bottleneck clearly. Its within-video localization quality is strong, with SVMR R@1 at IoU 0.5 equal to 15.60, but exhaustive corpus retrieval keeps VR R@1 at only 1.69 and VCMR R@1 at IoU 0.5 at 0.38. It also costs 7.36\,ms/query over the full validation sweep. This is exactly the regime where a staged pipeline should help: replace exhaustive retrieval with a shortlist that preserves useful candidates, then let \xml handle the fine-grained localization.

Our main reported operating point is dense top-5 shortlist $\rightarrow$ \xml. That pipeline raises VR R@1 from 1.69 to 11.56 and VCMR R@1 at IoU 0.5 from 0.38 to 1.43, while fully preserving SVMR R@1 at IoU 0.5 at 15.60. Runtime drops from 7.36\,ms/query for full \xml to 4.96\,ms/query, which is a 1.48$\times$ speedup and is now reported directly in \cref{tab:main-results}. The latency decomposition in \cref{tab:latency-breakdown} shows why this works: only about 0.39\,ms/query is spent on the dense front-end, while the dominant savings come from reducing the amount of expensive multimodal inference. Dense retrieval contributes the video-level recall, and \xml remains the component that delivers the final localization quality.

\subsection{Ablations}
We focus on the two ablations that directly test the \xml-stage design. The first is shortlist depth. Top-1 is the fastest shortlisted setting at 4.88\,ms/query and gives the best VCMR R@1 at IoU 0.5, namely 1.57, for a 1.51$\times$ speedup over full \xml. However, its shortlist video recall is only 11.56\%, which means the system is relying on a very narrow candidate set. Moving to top-5 almost doubles shortlist video recall to 22.29\% with only a 0.08\,ms/query increase in latency, while keeping VR R@1 at 11.56 and fully preserving SVMR R@1 at IoU 0.5. Top-10 pushes shortlist video recall further to 28.44\%, but its VCMR R@1 at IoU 0.5 does not improve over top-5 and the latency edge shrinks to 1.46$\times$. For that reason, top-5 is the cleanest balanced operating point to highlight in the main report.

The second ablation is shared compaction as an alternative way to cut \xml cost. It is informative mainly as negative evidence. Plain shared compaction with keep ratio 0.6 drops SVMR R@1 at IoU 0.5 to 11.23 and is slower than full \xml at 11.11\,ms/query, or only 0.66$\times$ as fast. Adding the dense front-end before shared compaction recovers some corpus-level quality, but it still underperforms dense shortlist $\rightarrow$ \xml, reaching VCMR R@1 at IoU 0.5 of 1.17 and SVMR R@1 at IoU 0.5 of 11.56 at 10.46\,ms/query, which is still only a 0.70$\times$ speedup factor. This means the compaction plumbing works, but the current selector is too crude to compete with hierarchical dense pruning. The strongest systems result in this repo is therefore the staged dense shortlist $\rightarrow$ \xml pipeline rather than shared compaction.
